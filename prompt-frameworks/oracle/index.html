ORACLE prompt framework

Objective, Role, Action, Context, Layout/Length, Example — A well-rounded, six-part structure for everyday prompts.

When to use ORACLE

ORACLE is the default choice when you're not sure which framework to reach for. It covers the six things almost any prompt benefits from: what you want, who the AI should act as, what to actually do, the background it needs, how the output should be shaped, and a concrete example. Use it for blog intros, emails, explainers, summaries, and general writing or research tasks.

The ORACLE components

O — Objective

The end result you're after — the single sentence that describes success.

R — Role

The persona or expertise you want the AI to adopt while answering.

A — Action

The specific task the AI needs to perform, stated as a clear instruction.

C — Context

Background details, audience, or constraints the AI needs to get it right.

L — Layout/Length

The format, structure, and rough length of the output.

E — Example

A sample of the tone, style, or structure you want it to match.

Bad prompt vs. ORACLE-structured prompt

Bad prompt:

“Write about email marketing.”

Structured with ORACLE:

Objective: Get more small business owners to open our weekly newsletter.
Role: You are an email marketing strategist with experience writing for busy small business owners.
Action: Write three subject line options for our weekly newsletter.
Context: The newsletter covers one practical marketing tip per week. Our audience is time-poor and skeptical of hype.
Layout/Length: Three subject lines, each under 60 characters, no emojis.
Example: Similar in tone to "The 10-minute fix for your slowest page" — specific and low-hype.

What you get back

Three subject lines matching the brief, each specific rather than generic, each under the character limit, with a short one-line note on why each one works for a skeptical, time-poor reader.

Which AI models it works well with

ORACLE's plain, labeled-section format works well across ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini — it doesn't rely on any model-specific syntax, which is why PromptGeneratorLab uses it as the default framework.
